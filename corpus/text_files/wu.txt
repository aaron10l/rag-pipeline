Systematic identification of SH3 domain-mediated human protein–protein interactions by peptide array target screening
Chenggang Wu1*, Mike Haiting Ma1*, Kevin R. Brown2, Matt Geisler1, Lei Li1, Eve Tzeng1, Christina Y. H. Jia1, Igor Jurisica2 and Shawn S.-C. Li1
1 Department of Biochemistry and the Siebens-Drake Research Institute, Schulich School of Medicine and Dentistry, University of Western Ontario, London, Ontario, Canada
2 Ontario Cancer Institute, Northeast Structural Genomics Consortium, Toronto, Ontario, Canada
Systematic identification of direct protein–protein interactions is often hampered by difficulties in expressing and purifying the corresponding full-length proteins. By taking advantage of the modular nature of many regulatory proteins, we attempted to simplify protein–protein interac- tions to the corresponding domain-ligand recognition and employed peptide arrays to identify such binding events. A group of 12 Src homology (SH) 3 domains from eight human proteins (Swiss-Prot ID: SRC, PLCG1, P85A, NCK1, GRB2, FYN, CRK) were used to screen a peptide target array composed of 1536 potential ligands, which led to the identification of 921 binary interactions between these proteins and 284 targets. To assess the efficiency of the peptide array target screening (PATS) method in identifying authentic protein–protein interactions, we exam- ined a set of interactions mediated by the PLCg1 SH3 domain by coimmunoprecipitation and/or affinity pull-downs using full-length proteins and achieved a 75% success rate. Furthermore, we characterized a novel interaction between PLCg1 and hematopoietic progenitor kinase 1 (HPK1) identified by PATS and demonstrated that the PLCg1 SH3 domain negatively regulated HPK1 kinase activity. Compared to protein interactions listed in the online predicted human interaction protein database (OPHID), the majority of interactions identified by PATS are novel, suggesting that, when extended to the large number of peptide interaction domains encoded by the human genome, PATS should aid in the mapping of the human interactome.

1 Introduction
 A description of the global protein connectivity in a cell is of enormous value to our understanding of all essential cellular functions and disease mechanisms [1–3]. Until recently, ge- nome-wide mapping of protein interactions, or the inter- actome, has been focused on model organisms such as Sac- charomyces cerevisiae[4–6], Caenorhabditis elegans [7], and Drosophila melanogaster [8]. throughput ability of the affinity purification coupled with MS (AP-MS) approach and systematic yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) [9], interactome frameworks have been established for these organisms, which in turn have provided unprece- dented insights into many important biological processes [10]. Recent work on large-scale identification of human protein interactions by Y2H has, however, changed the land- scape of the human interactome drastically [11, 12]. These studies outlined a skeleton for part of the human inter- actome and served as an impetus for more comprehensive mapping of the human interactome [13].
Although the current state of knowledge does not allow a thorough comparison between the human interactome and that of a model organism such as S. cerevisiae, for which a plethora of genetic, mass spectrometric, and Y2H data are available [14–19], the human interactome is believed to be more complex than that of the yeast. On one hand, the two species differ significantly in proteome size, cellular diversity, and compartmentalization; on the other hand, protein struc- tures vary considerably from S. cerevisiae to Homo sapiens [20– 22]. Compared to yeast, there is a drastic expansion of modular domains, especially the so-called peptide or protein interaction domains (PIDs), in the human genome. For instance, an esti- mated 120 copies of the Src-homology (SH) 2 domains are encoded by the human genome while no functional SH2 do- main has so far been identified in yeast [23]. Consequently, signaling events regulated by tyrosine phosphorylation and dephosphorylation do not take place in yeast. Similarly, a hu- man cell harbors approximately 300 SH3 domains while yeast contains only 28 copies of the same domain family [24].
Protein–protein interactions occurring via the recogni- tion of a conserved sequence motif in one protein by a mod- ular domain in another are a common means employed by a cell to assemble complex protein networks seen in signal transduction and underlie specific substrate recognition by protein kinases and phosphatases [25–27]. Several important features of interaction domains make them ideal targets also for large-scale human interactome mapping. First, interac- tion domains are found in thousands of human proteins. Second, they range from 30 to 150 amino acids in size, ap- proximately one-third of a typical human protein, and gen- erally fold into stable 3-D structures in solution. These char- acteristics render interaction domains particularly amenable to biochemical and biophysical manipulations. Third, many PIDs bind short peptide motifs as well as they do the corre- sponding native proteins [28]. Therefore, by identifying spe- cific peptides to which a PID binds, one can often deduce potential interacting proteins for that domain [29].
An SH3 domain-mediated protein interacting network was first identified in yeast using a strategy that combined specificity determination by phage display libraries with experimental screening for binary protein–protein interac- tions by Y2H [16]. Peptides synthesized on NC membranes were employed recently to map the binding partners for a group of yeast SH3 domains and to scan the human pro- teome [30]. Similarly, a proteome-wide phosphopeptide
membrane array was used in the discovery of potential Cdc4 substrates in yeast [31]. However, due to significant differ- ences in genome size and protein architecture between humans and yeast, most human protein interactions, espe- cially those occurring in signal transduction and cell–cell communication, may not be directly inferred from the yeast interactome. Indeed, a comparative analysis of over 70 000 binary interactions identified to date for yeast, worm, fly, and human revealed that only 16 interactions are common to all four species [32], suggesting that networks of protein–protein interactions differ significantly from one species to another. Because most of the regulatory proteins in humans have a modular architecture, domain-mediated interactions may constitute a significant part of the regulatory networks found in a human cell. Thus, high-throughput strategies that make use of these unique features provide attractive means to experimentally map regulatory networks in humans. For instance, MS-aided protein identification was applied to the mammalian WW and 14-3-3 domain families to identify their respective interaction networks [33, 34]. These studies revealed that modular domains connect multiple proteins in a network and are involved in regulating a wide range of cel- lular processes. Recently, domain macroarrays were employed to quantitatively characterize a protein interaction network mediated by the ErbB receptor [35]. In the present study, we applied a peptide array target screening (PATS) strategy to map interactions stemmed from a group of 12 SH3 domains taken from eight human proteins. The result- ing interaction network linked 8 “bait” proteins to 284 “tar- get” proteins through 921 binary interactions. Using PLCg1 as an example, we confirmed a number of PATS-derived interactions by in-solution peptide binding and affinity pull- down or coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays carried out on intact proteins. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the PLCg1 SH3 binds to the hematopoietic progenitor kinase 1 (HPK1) in vivo and thereby negatively regulates its kinase activity.

2.6 ComparisonofPATStotheonlinepredicted human interaction protein database (OPHID)
We compared PATS protein interactions to those found in the OPHID. The OPHID version used in this study contains 44800 unique interactions in which 1989 interactions
include at least one SH3 domain protein identified by PFAM-A [39]. The eight human SH3 domain-containing proteins analyzed by PATS (Swiss-Prot ID: SRC, PLCG1, P85A, NCK1, GRB2, FYN, CRK) have 626 interactions with 390 different proteins in OPHID. When these interactions were compared to the 921 interactions discovered by PATS, 35 direct matches were identified involving 21 different interacting proteins. An additional 28 SH3 domain-binding proteins were identified in interactions covered by both PATS and OPHID, but with different SH3 partners. The OPHID database included 8590 different human proteins, of which 724 were in common with the PATS “preys” and thus represent a common “prey” set. If we consider only the eight “bait” proteins used in PATS, and the 724 common “prey” proteins, then there are 566 interactions identified by PATS and 173 by OPHID. In order to apply the appropriate statis- tical analysis we considered the following: if all the possible interactions were balls in a bag, and we randomly draw out 566 balls, mark them, and return to the bag, then the prob- ability that we randomly choose a marked ball is 566/5792 for the first draw, and 566/(5792-1) for the second draw and so on. We thus calculated the probability for 173 sequential draws, which peaked at 17 marked balls (which is the expected overlap), and found that the probability of drawing 35 or more marked balls (the observed overlap between OPHID and PATS) is less than 1.9261025. Thus the observed intersection between OPHID and PATS databases is significant and cannot be explained by chance alone.
3 Results
3.1 Identification of an SH3 domain signaling network by PATS
The PATS approach is similar to the WISE method devel- oped by Landgraf et al. [30] for identifying a protein interac- tion network in yeast. Here we employed PATS to system- atically identify protein interactions mediated by a group of human SH3 domains. Because most SH3 domains select for an [R/K]xcPxxP (or the Class-I motif, where c represents a hydrophobic residue and x denotes any amino acid) or/and a PxcPx[R/K] (Class-II) motif [40], we treated these sequences as inputs to scan the Swiss-Prot protein database for peptides that contain such motifs. Some proteins such as collagens that are enriched in prolines for structural reasons were excluded from the list of retrieved peptides. A motif-scan- ning program developed in-house retrieved 768 Class-I pep- tides (each containing 13 residues) from 698 proteins and 768 Class-II peptides (containing 14 residues) from 670 pro- teins. For each peptide, the consensus motif was extended by three or four residues at both the N- and C-terminus to eliminate possible end effect. A total of 1536 peptides were synthesized in an array format on functionalized NC mem- branes. Fluorescent labeling allowed us to develop a simple assay to examine SH3–peptide interaction by incubating an SH3 domain with the peptide array and subsequently analyze the SH3–peptide complexes under a fluorescence imager. Selective binding of the PLCg1 SH3 domain to only a subset of pep- tides on the array is consistent with the notion that the Class I and II motifs are necessary, but not sufficient for SH3 binding. Because the dissociation constant (Kd) of a typical SH3–ligand complex falls within the range of 1–100 mM [40], we applied the SH3 domain at 1 mM to avoid signal satura- tion. The fluorescent signal produced by each peptide spot on the array was quantified and normalized against the average signal across the entire array. An exponential decay function was obtained when the normalized signals were plotted against the peptide spots (Fig. 1B), implying that binding was significant for only a small fraction of the pep- tides. This observation provided a rationale for the necessity to experimentally identify SH3-binding partners instead of relying solely on the computer-aided ligand prediction based on consensus motifs [29]. Using the transition point as a cut- off (Fig. 1B), we identified a group of peptides that bound significantly to the PLCg1 SH3 domain (Supporting Table S1). The same procedure was repeated for the remaining 11 SH3 domains.
Using the PATS strategy, we identified 1165 binary SH3– ligand interactions for the 12 human SH3 domains studied herein. Because a single SH3 domain could bind to multiple sites on a given prey protein, and on the other hand, multiple SH3 domains could prefer a single site, the number of SH3-binding proteins collapsed to 284 when these factors were taken into account. 3.2 IdentificationofaPLCa1interactionnetwork
The phosphoinositide-specific phospholipase C family of isozymes catalyze the conversion of phosphotidylinositol 4,5- bisphosphate (PIP2) to two second messengers, inositol- 1,4,5 phosphate (IP3) and diacylgylcerol (DAG) [42–46] that are involved in intracellular calcium release and protein kinase C activation, respectively [47]. The PLCg1 subtype, which is ubiquitously expressed, differs from the b and d types in that it contains an insertion in its catalytic domain. This insertion, consisting of two SH2 and one SH3 domains flanked by a split PH domain [42], is thought to play an important part in coupling PLCg1 to receptor activation [43]. Interestingly, the SH3 domain has been shown to function independently of the holoenzyme. For instance, agonist- induced Ca21 entry into PC12 cells requires the SH3 instead of the lipase domain of PLCg1 [48]. The mitogenic effect of PLCg1 was also ascribed to the SH3 domain because expres- sing the SH3 domain alone could induce mitogenesis in quiescent NIH3T3 cells [49, 50]. How does a small peptide- binding module accomplish the role of a mitogen? Since the SH3 domain lacks catalytic activity found in a typical en- zyme, it must exert its regulatory functions by associating with other molecules in the cell.
Using PATS, we identified 63 proteins that could poten- tially interact with PLCg1 through its SH3 domain (Sup- porting Table S1). These interacting proteins include 7 GTPases (e.g. R-Ras) and their regulators (e.g. GEFs and GAPs such as DDEF1/2, SOS1/2), eight kinases or phos- phatases (e.g. M4K1, Abl2, Jak2, DUS15), nine receptors (e.g. FcgR2C, IR3Ra), ten transcription factors or DNA/RNA- binding proteins (e.g. RAG-1, DLX4, AIRE), four transporters (e.g. RyR-1, SNX17), six other signaling molecules (e.g. SHAN2/3), and 19 unclassified proteins (Fig. 3). Impor- tantly, seven disease proteins were also identified as potential targets of the PLCg1 SH3 domain. These include the CUB and sushi multiple domains protein 2 (CSMD2), dystro- brevin binding protein 1 (DTBP1), autoimmune poly- endocrinopathy candidiasisectodermal dystrophy protein (AIRE), colonic and hepatic tumor overexpressed protein (CHTOG), Fanconi anemia group A protein (FANCA), fra- gile X mental retardation 2 protein (FMR2), and the glioma tumor suppressor candidate region gene 1 protein (GSCR1; Fig. 3). This result suggests that the PLCg1 SH3 domain may exert its function through a wide spectrum of regulatory pathways and that deregulation of one or more of these pathways may contribute to various diseases. 3.3 Validation of PATS interactions by in-solution binding assays
Because PATS measures binary domain–peptide interac- tions on a solid support, it is necessary to confirm PATS- derived interactions in solution using full-length proteins. Since it was impractical to confirm all the interactions iden- tified by PATS, we concentrated on validating interactions mediated by the PLCg1 SH3 domain. Subject to the avail- ability of cDNA clones, a representative group of 12 proteins identified by PATS as binding partners for the PLCg1 SH3 domain were tested for binding by GST pull-down or co-IP. These included five proteins (DDEF1/2, SOS1/2, and RIN3) in the “GTPase/GEF/GAP” category, two (M4K1 or HPK1 and Abl2) from “kinases/phosphatases”, one (FCG2B) from “receptors”, one (SNX17) from “transporters”, and two (SHAN2/3) representing “other signaling molecules” (Fig. 3). When expressed in HEK293T cells nine of the twelve pro- teins (75%) exhibited robust binding to either the SH3 do- main or intact PLCg1 (Fig. 4 and Table 1), demonstrating that PATS is capable of identifying authentic protein–protein interactions. Remarkably, eight of the nine proteins (except for SOS1) that displayed positive binding in solution are unknown interactors of PLCg1, suggesting that PATS is a powerful method for uncovering novel protein functions. 3.4 A novel interaction between HPK1 and PLCa1 identified by PATS.
To establish the physiological relevance of interactions revealed by PATS, one needs to carry out more focused studies on the corresponding protein pairs in a biological setting. To this end we chose to investigate a novel interac- tion between PLCg1 and HPK1 (or M4K1) in more detail. HPK1 is an STE20-like serine/threonine kinase expressed only in hematopoietic cells and tissue [38], and the mechan- ism for its regulation is not well understood. We first estab- lished that HPK1 and PLCg1 could interact in vivo. As shown in Fig. 5A, Flag-tagged PLCg1, but not a W828A mutant that disrupts the binding site of the SH3 domain, co-IP’ed with HA-tagged HPK1 when both proteins were expressed in HEK293T cells. Since crosslinking of the T or B cell receptors leads to HPK1 activation in lymphocytes, we next examined whether the interaction between the two proteins is coupled to receptor activation. Jurkat T cells were thus treated with an anti-CD3 antibody to activate the T cell receptor (verified by SLP-76 and LAT phosphorylation, data not shown) and endogenous PLCg1 was precipitated from both treated and untreated cells. As seen in Fig. 5B, endogenous HPK1 and PLCg1 can form a complex in T cells regardless of TCR acti- vation, suggesting the two proteins interact in a constitutive manner.

When comparing the PATS and OPHID interaction datasets, we identified 35 “exact matches” (both SH3 and binding proteins matched) involv- ing 21 unique proteins and an additional 28 cross-matched interactions in which the binding partners were linked to different SH3 proteins in the two datasets (Table 2). It is important to point out that all the matched interactions were mediated by the set of 184 proteins classified to func- tion in signal transduction and/or cell communication (Fig. 2), indicating that functional correlation underpins protein networking. Of the 35 matched interactions, 22 were verified in the literature to involve direct SH3-ligand binding (Supporting Table S3). Although interactions mediated by SH3 proteins constitute only a small part of the OPHID database at present, the overlap between OPHID and PATS is highly significant (p = 1.92E-05). Apart from these matches, the majority of interactions identified by PATS were novel. In particular, 550 binary interactions (that are not listed in OPHID) were identified from this study, suggesting that PATS is capable of not only recapitulating known interactions, but also unraveling novel interactions and is therefore highly complementary to existing high- throughput methods of network mapping. Nevertheless, it should be cautioned that it is likely that a significant num- ber of SH3-mediated interactions listed in OPHID were overlooked by PATS. This so-called false negative rate is, however, difficult to evaluate since OPHID (and for that matter, any current, large protein–protein interaction data- base) only lists interacting proteins with no (or limited) information on whether the interaction involves a particular domain or where the binding occurs. In this regard, PATS may help annotate current protein–protein interaction databases by providing binding site information.
4 Discussion
We described the application of a peptide-array-based meth- od to systematically identify potential protein–protein inter- actions mediated by a group of human SH3 domains. PATS or WISE [30] simplifies protein–protein interaction to do- main-ligand recognition, and thereby represents a versatile method that can be used to identify binary interactions and the corresponding binding sites simultaneously. Combining the PATS and the OPHID datasets, the eight proteins exam- ined in this study generated a total of 1517 binary protein– protein interactions. Notably, in our limited case study with PLCg1, up to 75% of these interactions can be recapitulated on full-length proteins, suggesting that PATS is a useful method to identify authentic protein–protein interactions.

It is conceivable that PATS may be extended to the remainder of the human SH3 domain family to identify the corresponding SH3-mediated interaction network, or to other PIDs such as the WW and EVH1 domains that recognize proline-rich sequences [40]. Although PATS could potentially be used to map networks mediated by phosphopeptide-binding domains such as SH2, PTB, FHA, and 14-3-3 [25], the corresponding peptide sequence may not be as readily accessible as a proline-rich motif in a folded protein. In addition, the phosphorylation potential of each peptide has to be evaluated [29] before PATS can be applied, otherwise the number of potential targets to be screened would be too large to manipulate in a cost-effec- tive manner.
It should be recognized that not all protein–protein interactions are mediated by domain binding to short pep- tide sequences. Indeed, even for SH3 domains, many recog- nize amino acids far removed from the consensus binding site, and some SH3–ligand interactions depend on 3-D sur- face–surface contacts instead of motif recognition [55]. Moreover, certain SH3–ligand interactions are rather weak and take place only in specific biological settings or when in cooperation with other domains present in the same mole- cule [40]. Apparently these types of interactions will be over- looked in the current design of PATS.
Despite these considerations, we expect PATS to play an important part in deciphering the human interactome. It is possible that interactions mediated by modular domains (such as SH3) and unmodified peptides (such as Pro-rich motifs) provide a skeleton of basal protein–protein interac- tion networks on which dynamic interactions elicited by PTMs (such as phosphorylation) can be added in response to specific environmental cues. Large-scale mapping of net- works mediated by interaction domains and related bioin- formatics analysis should help establish the fundamental principles of protein network organization in humans and other metazoan species. Finally, it should be pointed out that PATS is an in vitro method that maps potential, static pro- tein–protein interactions. Only when it is combined with other orthogonal methods of network mapping and charac- terization of network dynamics, will its full potential be real- ized.
